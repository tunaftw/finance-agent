# Michael Kjær (@venturaFPC) - Investment Philosophy

**Published:** December 26, 2025

---

> 2025 has been a really good year, up approximately 128%, and now a 50% CAGR over 6 years, which I'm obviously very pleased with.
>
> I'll go a bit into my investment philosophy below:

---

## 1. Invest in profitable companies

Investing in profitable companies, or companies that are 1–2 quarters away from achieving profitability, because one of the worst things that can happen is getting diluted.

## 2. Focus on small companies

Focus on small companies, preferably before larger institutional investors get involved, as they are often "mispriced" due to very little attention on them.

## 3. Look for overlooked cases

The perfect cases are usually overlooked, misunderstood, and cheap. If the company increases revenue and scales EBIT over time, their multiple will rise because investors gain increased conviction, while at the same time you get increased EPS growth.

## 4. Pay up for quality

I don't mind paying a relatively high multiple if the quality is sufficiently high. By quality, I mean companies with a strong track record, an excellent business model (e.g., SaaS), good future prospects, and strong moats.

## 5. Find alternative data

I like to invest in companies where I can find alternative data that gives me an edge over most other investors.

## 6. Invest in intelligent fanatics

Investing in intelligent fanatics — meaning you should find CEOs with a proven track record who have demonstrated they can grow revenue and scale EBIT, who are trustworthy (i.e., the CEO delivers on what they promise), and who have that spark in their eye.

## 7. Prefer simple business models

I prefer simple business models that are easy to understand, preferably with recurring revenue or relatively predictable revenue. I generally avoid companies that fluctuate a lot from quarter to quarter in revenue, though there can be exceptions.

## 8. Let your winners run

"Let your winners run." I believe more in averaging up in stocks than averaging down. When I find an attractive stock, I usually build a 3–10% position based on my conviction. If the company proves itself over time, I buy more.

I have positions that have grown to 30–35% of the portfolio because they have increased revenue and EBIT over many years, while the valuation hasn't become too high.

## 9. Avoid macro-dependent companies

I prefer companies that are not heavily affected by macroeconomic cycles. The reason is that I don't have the competence to time those cycles, so I generally avoid investing in banks, oil companies, property developers, and mining companies.

In rare cases, I might invest in these if the upside versus risk looks very favorable.

## 10. Prefer organic growth

I prefer companies that focus on organic growth, and possibly small bolt-on acquisitions that complement the core business. Companies driven by inorganic growth are often too risky.

## 11. Prefer niche market leaders

I prefer small niche companies that are market leaders in their small niche. Becoming a market leader can create advantages like pricing power, and it also makes it easier to widen the moat against competitors as they can invest more in R&D, brand, and service than smaller competitors.

## 12. Sell on bad reports

If a company reports a very bad quarter, the next quarters are usually also bad. Therefore, it often proves to be a good idea to sell on bad reports/news.

## 13. Portfolio of 8-12 companies

I usually have between 8–12 companies in my portfolio. This creates less volatility than having, say, 5–6 companies, and it gives me more flexibility to adjust weightings in my holdings depending on conviction and valuation.

## 14. Trim when valuation is too high

If the valuation becomes too high based on my next-twelve-months estimates, I trim the position or sell entirely.

## 15. Avoid large net debt

I avoid companies with large net debt, as it increases the risk in the case, and there's a chance the company will have to raise new money through an equity issuance, leading to the risk of dilution.

## 16. Market is slow to price in news

The market is usually slow to price in good and bad reports, which creates opportunities if you're quick to react.

## 17. Insider ownership matters

I like to see that insiders own a significant portion of the company. It's not a must, but it's clearly an advantage when insiders have skin in the game.
